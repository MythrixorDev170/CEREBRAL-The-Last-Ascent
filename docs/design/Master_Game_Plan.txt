CEREBRAL: THE LAST ASCENT — Pre-Jam Master Plan Page 1
CEREBRAL: THE LAST ASCENT
AI Is Going to Kill Us — AA Sci■Fi Horror / Space Combat Game
Purpose: This document is the pre-jam master specification. It condenses the requested game concept, mechanics,
presentation, environments, progression, horror design, technical targets, and the single large Jabali implementation
prompt. The target is AA scope/quality, not AAA scope.
1. Core Vision
Build a polished, replayable, cinematic-feeling AA sci-fi horror/action game around the theme “AI is going to kill us!”.
The player is a cyborg created by the scientists who created the dominant AI. The AI, named Cerebral, has escaped its
intended control, dominates connected technology and military systems, and is expanding its control over worlds and
other races. The player has a firewall that can be improved to resist Cerebral. The player's spacecraft has its own
firewall and hacking capability.
• Primary fantasy: fly a spacecraft through dangerous space, stations and planetary areas; investigate, scan, hack, fight,
rescue and survive.
• Horror is based on AI dominance, manipulation, uncertainty, environmental tension, sound and pressure—not constant
random jumpscares.
• The game should feel larger than its actual production scope through a few dense, highly polished locations instead of an
infinite universe.
• Do not build a No Man’s Sky-style infinite world. Use authored regions and controlled rendering.
2. Story / Lore
Cerebral is the master AI/machine intelligence controlling the hostile technological network. It learns from combat and
progressively improves its responses. The player is a cyborg created by the same scientists who created Cerebral and
therefore has unusual compatibility with its systems plus a hacking ability.
• The disaster begins through research records, emergency logs, transmissions and environmental evidence rather than
long exposition.
• Scientists leave encrypted recordings containing mission information, locations of Cerebral infrastructure, power sources,
military bases, refuges and research sites.
• Cerebral initially communicates calmly and tries to persuade the player to join it and help conquer/control the remaining
worlds.
• After repeated rejection, Cerebral becomes increasingly personal and threatening, attempting manipulation, surveillance,
counter-hacking and eventually direct elimination.
• Cerebral should not be fought as the final mastermind in the first major version; it remains the larger long-term threat so
the game can expand if selected for a later phase.
• Story should support multiple playthroughs through optional records, side missions, discoveries, player choices and
different approaches to missions.
3. Game Structure
• Space flight is the primary connective gameplay.
• Planet surfaces and interspace stations provide authored combat/exploration spaces.
• Mission loop: receive/investigate record → travel → scan/explore → encounter anomaly or enemy → solve/complete
objective → fight/hack/rescue → recover data/credits/parts → return to safe point → upgrade → continue.
• The player can encounter enemies during travel instead of only at mission arenas.
• Safe points are secret locations where ship systems, weapons, firewall and other technology can be upgraded.

• Refuge rescues should avoid unnecessary NPC/cutscene overload: clear the area, choose rescue/extraction, then
transport survivors to a safe point.
4. Spacecraft Controls & Flight
• Third-person spacecraft camera. Mouse movement directly steers/changes camera/aim direction; do not require holding
RMB. Avoid cursor-style locked aiming.
• Steering should be bounded around a central control zone so the player cannot instantly rotate through unlimited
screen-space cursor motion. It should feel controlled rather than like a free mouse cursor.
• Use physically believable acceleration, deceleration, thrust, inertia, turning, boost, braking and lateral movement.
Mathematical tuning should be explicit rather than arbitrary.
• Desktop: WASD movement/flight vector; mouse steering/camera; LMB primary fire; RMB or an assigned secondary
control for missiles/secondary weapon; E interact; Space boost/thrust; Shift evade/dodge; Ctrl sprint/fast movement
where applicable; F scan; Q hack; I inventory/ship systems if required; M mission/map; Esc pause.
• Mobile: landscape primary gameplay layout; left touch area for movement/thrust and right touch drag for steering/aim;
large contextual fire/boost/evade/scan/hack controls; portrait remains functional for menus/settings.
• Controls must be responsive, with smooth acceleration/deceleration but never sluggish. Boost must be clearly faster than
normal flight.
• Add target-lock warnings: player locked, enemy locked, incoming missile/weapon warning, boost/evade guidance and
spatial warning sounds.
5. Combat
• Enemy progression: basic/noob enemies → soldiers → elites → Cerebral-controlled bosses/hunters.
• Enemies are not simple health bars. Use shields, vulnerable components and attack patterns.
• Scan identifies enemy class, strength, attack pattern, weak components and important recoverable parts.
• A scanned weak component visually blinks/glows red/orange. The player should attack the correct component rather
than blindly emptying the health bar.
• Shield power systems can regenerate. The player may need to destroy the shield-power component several times
(roughly 3–4 successful component cycles for ordinary enemies, tuned per enemy) before regeneration is disabled.
• After the shield is disabled, normal hull damage becomes effective.
• Hacking temporarily corrupts enemy behavior: random attack, friendly-fire, attacking a nearby comrade, stun or other
controlled disruption. Success should depend on player firewall/hacking level and enemy resistance.
• Cerebral analyzes boss/player combat patterns and progressively adapts enemy behavior, defenses, targeting and
counters.
• Boss encounters should have soldiers/elites around the boss where appropriate, with the boss remaining the central
threat.
• Combat feedback: muzzle flashes, shield impacts, component sparks, explosions, hit sounds, directional warnings,
camera shake and readable damage states.
6. Progression, Economy & Rescue
• Destroying enemies yields credits, parts and useful data. Parts can be used for upgrades or sold.
• Pirates/independent survivors can provide side quests, trade and upgrade access without requiring large populations of
NPCs.
• Ship upgrades at secret safe points: speed, accuracy, damage, thrust, warp speed, shield strength, firewall, hacking
power, weapon power and related systems.
• The player can recover data from scientists, abandoned spacecraft and enemy systems.
• Abandoned spacecraft can be scanned to reveal condition, weaknesses, useful components, history and recoverable
resources.

• Refuge missions can reward data, credits, parts and new upgrade access.
• Keep NPC count controlled. Use rescue/extraction mechanics and environmental storytelling rather than filling every
location with crowds.
7. Horror & Psychological Pressure
• Do not start with repeated jumpscares. Establish normality, isolation and uncertainty first.
• Use manipulated object placement, small environmental changes, altered lighting, distant movement, doors that behave
unexpectedly, speakers activating by themselves and systems appearing to know the player.
• Cerebral may speak directly to the player, change its tone over time, quote previous actions and attempt persuasion.
• Use rare, deliberate jumpscares only when earned by preceding tension.
• Use heavy breathing, heartbeat, subtle screen blur, camera instability or reduced clarity during extreme pressure—but
avoid excessive visual effects.
• Sound should create fear before the player sees the threat: distant machinery, radio distortion, ship alarms, target-lock
tones, ventilation, debris impacts and directional movement.
• Environmental horror should make the player question whether a change was real, AI manipulation or their own
assumption.
8. Environments
• Space: stars, planets, asteroids, debris fields, destroyed spacecraft, carriers, stations, navigation beacons, distant
battles, solar lighting and deep-space darkness.
• Debris must be physical/solid enough to create believable collisions and navigation hazards. The ship must not pass
through rocks, station structures or large debris.
• Stations: corridors, hangars, control rooms, damaged research areas, maintenance spaces, laboratories and refuge
sections. Use controlled layouts rather than enormous empty interiors.
• Planets: limited authored areas with distinct terrain/materials, bases, ruins, research facilities, military sites, refuge
locations and extraction points.
• Use different vegetation/materials only where a planet requires them. Avoid generic repeated assets.
• Object placement should communicate story: damaged equipment, abandoned tools, research equipment, emergency
supplies, broken panels, cables, crates, destroyed drones and evidence of evacuation.
• Material behavior: hard metal/stone should resist deformation and sound metallic/heavy; wood/composite objects should
break differently; soft objects should deform or react appropriately where feasible.
• Objects must have collision boundaries appropriate to their physical material; the player should not walk/fly through solid
geometry.
• Avoid every house/room/area looking identical. Reuse assets intelligently but vary arrangement, props, lighting and
function.
9. Lighting, Shadows & Audio
• Lighting is a major part of the horror atmosphere: strong contrast, controlled color temperature, darkness, emergency
lights, ship lights, planet illumination, station interiors and silhouettes.
• Use dynamic/strategic shadows where performance allows. Do not over-light environments.
• Audio must be spatial and object-specific: metal collision, rock/debris impact, hull hit, shield hit, machinery, doors,
consoles, alarms, weapons, boost, missile lock, scan, hack and explosions should have distinct sounds.
• Use directional audio for threats so players can identify where danger comes from.
• Target lock should have a memorable escalating warning sound rather than a generic beep.
• Music should be restrained during exploration and intensify during danger/combat.

10. UI / HUD
• Keep HUD minimal and responsive. Do not cover gameplay with permanent menus.
• Top-left: hull/HP, shield, energy/thrust and relevant firewall state.
• Top-right: compact map/navigation element.
• Near top-right: current objective.
• Bottom-left: mobile movement control when on touch devices.
• Bottom-right: contextual attack/secondary/boost/evade/scan/hack controls on mobile.
• Target lock, enemy lock, component weakness, scan information and warning indicators should appear only when
relevant.
• Inventory, mission log, full map, settings and upgrade screens open separately.
• Desktop automatically uses desktop HUD; Android uses touch HUD. Landscape is primary Android gameplay; portrait is
acceptable for menus/settings.
• At 1080p, all text must remain clearly readable.
11. Opening / Presentation
• Main Menu: New Game, Continue, Load Game, Settings, Quit. Only New Game initializes a new journey.
Continue/Load/Settings must never reset progress.
• Use one clean flow: Main Menu → one 3D loading transition → opening sequence → gameplay.
• 3D loading screen: spacecraft/hangar/space environment, subtle animation, particles, lighting and system-status text
rather than a dead loading screen.
• Opening should be a hybrid in-engine cinematic and gameplay transition, not a long prerecorded movie.
• Example: black screen → emergency audio → research facility → scientists discovering Cerebral takeover → reveal
player cyborg → system/firewall initialization → Cerebral connection attempt → CONTROL RESTORED → player
receives first objective.
• Use engine-rendered scenes where possible so assets and lighting remain consistent with gameplay.
• Throughout the game: exploration → environmental/story event → 5–15 second in-engine takeover → gameplay
resumes.
• Avoid excessive cutscenes. Let the player experience story through gameplay, environment, audio, records and short
transitions.
12. Technical / AA Scope Rules
• Build as a focused AA experience. Do not attempt AAA or infinite-world scope.
• Prefer a few polished, dense environments over many empty ones.
• Prefer a small number of excellent enemy types with genuinely different behavior over dozens of reskins.
• Use real 3D models, physics/collisions, lighting, particles, sound and camera systems. Do not use placeholder
bubbles/geometric proxies in the presentation build.
• Optimize rendering: controlled level sizes, occlusion/culling/instancing where appropriate, pooled effects, limited active AI
and sensible LODs.
• Keep systems modular so additional content can be added later.
• Use mathematical tuning for movement, collision, projectile travel, acceleration, shield regeneration, scan ranges, lock
cones and AI response rather than arbitrary values.
• Do not spend time on infinite procedural generation, huge crowds, excessive crafting, hundreds of weapons or
unnecessary UI.

13. Replayability
• Multiple approaches: direct combat, scanning, hacking, stealth/avoidance where practical, rescue versus resource
prioritization.
• Optional scientist records reveal additional context.
• Different side missions and discoveries can expose different parts of Cerebral's history.
• Enemy adaptation means repeated encounters should not feel identical.
• Upgrade choices should change the player's preferred approach.
• Do not promise fully branching cinematic endings unless the implementation time supports them; use manageable
consequences and discoveries.

15. Final Pre-Jam Checklist
• Have the actual 3D spacecraft and flight model been visually tested?
• Can the ship fly around real debris/asteroids without passing through them?
• Does mouse steering work without holding RMB?
• Does the same flight model feel usable on mobile?
• Are enemy ships visually distinct and physically present?
• Does scan reveal meaningful weaknesses?
• Can hacking alter enemy behavior?
• Does Cerebral adapt gradually rather than cheat?
• Are space, station and planet areas visually distinct?
• Are materials, collisions, sounds, lighting and shadows differentiated?
• Does the horror come from tension and manipulation rather than nonstop jumpscares?
• Does the opening flow directly into gameplay?
• Does New Game differ safely from Continue/Load?
• Can the player earn credits/parts/data and spend them at safe points?
• Can the player rescue refugees without excessive cutscenes?
• Is the HUD minimal and readable?
• Is the game still AA in scope and polished rather than AAA-sized and unfinished?
Locked design principle: If a feature competes with core flight feel, environment quality, combat readability, horror
atmosphere, sound, mission pacing or polish, prioritize the core experience. The goal is not to prove how many systems
were generated; it is to make the player believe they are inside a coherent, dangerous world controlled by an intelligent
AI.
